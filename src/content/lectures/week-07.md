## Guiding question

> What can you know when most of the map is invisible?

## Vision is an information problem, not a placement guide

This week is not about where to put wards. It's about what a team can
actually claim to know at a given moment, and what follows from admitting
the rest is uncertain.

## Five kinds of (non-)knowledge

- **directly observed** — currently visible: ground truth, right now
- **inferred** — reasoned from indirect evidence; all of Week 5's jungle
  tracking is this
- **missing** — genuinely unknown, with no direct or circumstantial evidence
  yet available
- **stale** — was observed, isn't current, and degrades in reliability the
  longer it's been since the observation was made
- **information denial** — actively preventing the opponent from directly
  observing you: clearing wards, pathing around vision, timing a play to
  outlast a ward's duration

Uncertainty is what's left over after sorting through all five. It isn't a
failure state to be eliminated — it's the permanent condition decisions get
made under.

## What a ward actually buys

Not "vision" as an abstract good. A ward changes which actions are safe (a
gank threat stops mattering once its approach is visible), which threats
are credible (an all-in only works if the target doesn't see it coming),
and which parts of the map a team can commit to with confidence rather than
a guess. Week 6's tempo only pays off if the information behind the
decision to spend it hasn't gone stale by the time it's spent.

## Builds directly on Weeks 5–6

Week 5 practised inference without naming it as a category; this week gives
it the same structure explicitly — observed, inferred, missing, stale — and
applies it past the jungle to the whole map. And a tempo decision (Week 6)
made on stale information is a bet, not a plan: the window it was aimed at
may already be gone.

## Map-state exercise

You're told:

- your team currently has vision on the enemy top laner and on the
  blue-side red-buff camp, both empty
- the enemy jungler has not appeared on any ward for the last ninety
  seconds
- your bot lane's wave is slow-pushing toward the enemy's own tower
- Dragon spawns in 40 seconds

Sort this into four buckets before deciding anything:

1. **What do we know?** Top lane's state, directly observed. The red-buff
   camp is clear, directly observed.
2. **What can we reasonably infer?** The enemy jungler is plausibly
   dragon-side, given the timer and the incentive their team has to contest
   it — and your slow-pushing bot wave gives them an additional reason to
   be down there rather than elsewhere.
3. **What do we not know?** Whether they're already in the river. Whether
   their team is grouping to join them.
4. **What decision follows despite that?** Send appropriate support or
   vision toward dragon-side before letting your bot carry commit alone to
   the pushed wave — the cost of being wrong about a pick under an
   unwarded bush is worse than the cost of contesting a wave slightly
   later.

This is exactly the shape of reasoning Assessment 2 asks for, at length,
against a real partial game state.
